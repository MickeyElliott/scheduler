## The standard you're training for
### BLEA Entrance PAT (pass/fail gate)
| Event | Standard | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Push-ups | 20 correct reps in 90 s | Then 3:00 rest |
| Sit-ups | 25 correct reps in 90 s | Then 5:00 rest |
| Squat thrusts | 35 correct reps in 3:00 | Fail any event = fail PAT |
- Push-up: body straight, go down until upper arms are parallel and chest compresses the foam block, lock arms out at the top.
- Sit-up: fingers interlocked behind head, elbows touch knees, shoulder blades return to the mat.
- Squat thrust: hands to floor, kick both feet back together to a straight plank, feet back in, stand fully with arms overhead.
- Rep standards above are as published by WSCJTC/NTN. Confirm with the FWPD proctor which standard they score against.
_Source: WSCJTC: https://cjtc.wa.gov/physical-ability-test-pat_

### VSWAT Fitness Assessment (ranked)
| Event | Standard | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Part 1, 10:00 AMRAP | 5 pull-ups / 10 push-ups / 15 air squats | then 3:00 rest |
| Part 2, 3:00 AMRAP | 5 deadlifts @ 75% BW (≈135 lb at 180) / 5 burpees | then 3:00 rest |
| Part 3, 3:00 AMRAP | 5 deadlifts @ 75% BW / 5 burpees | Ranked on score |
- Rep standards for pull-ups (kip vs. strict), burpees (chest to floor? jump?) and deadlift lockout are not given in the announcement. Ask a current Valley SWAT member or the test proctor. Until then, train strict pull-ups and chest-to-floor burpees.
- Deadlift load = 75% of YOUR weigh-in body weight. If you lose 10 lb the bar drops to about 127.5 lb. Train at 135 lb either way.

### Pull-up progression (set your level from the baseline max)
| Level | Session prescription | Grease the groove |
|---|---|---|
| L0: 0 strict | Band-assisted pull-ups 5×5 (band light enough that rep 5 is hard) + 3×3 eccentric negatives (jump up, 5 s lower) + 3×20 s flexed-arm hang + inverted rows 3×8-10. | Negatives: 3-5 singles spread across the day |

| L3: 10+ strict | EMOM 10: 5 strict pull-ups (test-standard reps). Add 10 lb in a backpack once all 10 minutes are clean. | Sets of 4-5, 4-6 times/day |

### AMRAP pacing strategy
- 10-min AMRAP: the pull-ups set your pace. From round 1, break them before failure (3-2, then 2-2-1 later). One failed rep costs more time than a planned break.
- Push-ups: sets of 6-4 or 5-5 from the start. Never take a set to failure in rounds 1-4.
- Air squats: always unbroken. Breathe in through the nose on the squats and use them to recover.
- Pick a target round time from your baseline (e.g. 75 s/round = 8 rounds) and hold it ±5 s. Check the clock every round. If rounds 1-2 are more than 10 s faster than target, you went out too hot.
- 3-min DL/burpee: deadlifts touch-and-go, reset the grip only if needed. Burpees at a smooth, steady rhythm, ~2.5-3 s each. Walk to the bar, don't rest at it. Target ≈ 25-30 s/round = 6-7 rounds.
- The 3:00 rests: walk the first 60 s, take nasal breaths, chalk up, set up at the bar with 20 s left.
- The second DL/burpee AMRAP is where rankings are decided. Plan to match or beat your first-AMRAP score.

### Deadlift to 135: cues
- Bar over mid-foot, shins about 1 in from the bar.
- Hinge down and grip just outside the legs. Shins come to the bar. Chest up, 'bend the bar' to engage the lats.
- Big breath into the belly, brace, push the floor away. Hips and shoulders rise together.
- Lock out with glutes, not by leaning back. Lower by sending the hips back first, bar close to the legs.
- Stop the set if your lower back rounds or the bar drifts forward. Technique comes before load in week 1.
- For the test, use touch-and-go reps (no bouncing) and a double-overhand grip as long as it holds, then switch to mixed grip.

### Dry-fire safety rules (every session)
1. Use a dedicated dry-fire area with a backstop that would stop a round (exterior wall/body-armor panel/bookcase), never a wall people are behind.

## 4 · Case law (Week 1)
Method: read the holding, write 3–5 bullets, then say 'why it matters' out loud as if answering the interview panel. All citations were checked against the opinions or official reporters.

### Graham v. Connor · 490 U.S. 386 (1989) · _Mon 11/30_
**Holding.** All claims that officers used excessive force during an arrest, investigatory stop or other seizure are analyzed under the Fourth Amendment's 'objective reasonableness' standard, not substantive due process. Reasonableness is judged from the perspective of a reasonable officer on the scene, not with 20/20 hindsight, and allows for split-second judgments in tense, uncertain and rapidly evolving situations. The officer's subjective intent is irrelevant.

The three Graham factors: (1) severity of the crime at issue, (2) whether the suspect poses an immediate threat to the safety of officers or others, (3) whether the suspect is actively resisting or attempting to evade arrest by flight.

**Why it matters for SWAT.** Every use-of-force articulation in a SWAT report or interview has to map to Graham. Fast-moving entries are the 'tense, uncertain, rapidly evolving' setting the Court described, but WA's RCW 10.120 adds 'necessary' + reasonable care on top of Graham.

**Self-quiz:** Name the three Graham factors and the vantage point a court must use.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Severity of the crime, immediate threat, active resistance/flight. Judged as a reasonable officer on scene, without hindsight, allowing for split-second decisions.</details>

Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/490/386/

### Tennessee v. Garner · 471 U.S. 1 (1985) · _Mon 11/30_
**Holding.** Using deadly force to prevent escape is a Fourth Amendment seizure. It may not be used unless it is necessary to prevent the escape AND the officer has probable cause to believe the suspect poses a significant threat of death or serious physical injury to the officer or others. Where feasible, some warning must be given. The Court held the Tennessee statute unconstitutional as applied to an unarmed, non-dangerous fleeing burglary suspect.

**Why it matters for SWAT.** This governs fleeing-felon situations on perimeters and containment, e.g. a 'squirter' leaving a target house. WA adds RCW 9A.16.040 (criminal) and RCW 10.120.020(2) (civil 'immediate threat' standard), and FWPD adopts all three (MOS 4.1.3).

**Self-quiz:** When may deadly force be used against a fleeing suspect under Garner? What about a warning?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Only if necessary to prevent escape and there's probable cause the suspect poses a significant threat of death/serious injury to officers or others. Give a warning where feasible.</details>

Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/471/1/

### Barnes v. Felix · 605 U.S. 73 (2025) · _Tue 12/1_
**Holding.** The Court was unanimous (May 15, 2025). It rejected the Fifth Circuit's 'moment-of-threat' rule, which looked only at the instant the officer was in danger. Excessive-force claims get a totality-of-the-circumstances analysis that 'has no time limit': earlier events, including the reasons for the stop and prior interactions, can bear on whether the force was reasonable. The Court expressly did NOT decide whether or how an officer's own creation of the danger factors in.

**Why it matters for SWAT.** Your whole operation is on the table: the planning, the approach, the breach and the call-out, not just the trigger press. It lines up with WA's 'totality of the circumstances' definition (RCW 10.120.010(8)), which includes the officer's own actions. That's why sound tactics count legally as well as operationally.

**Self-quiz:** What did Barnes reject, and what question did it leave open?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>It rejected the moment-of-threat doctrine and held that totality has no time limit. It left open how an officer-created danger factors into reasonableness.</details>

Source: https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/24pdf/23-1239_onjq.pdf

### Scott v. Harris (supplemental) · 550 U.S. 372 (2007) · _Tue 12/1_
**Holding.** An officer's attempt to end a dangerous high-speed chase that threatens innocent bystanders does not violate the Fourth Amendment, even when it puts the fleeing motorist at risk of serious injury or death. Where video 'blatantly contradicts' the plaintiff's version, courts view the facts as shown in the video. There is no 'magical on/off switch' test for deadly force; reasonableness is fact-bound.

**Why it matters for SWAT.** Federal law is permissive here, but WA law is much tighter: RCW 10.116.060 (pursuit criteria + no shooting at moving vehicles unless the vehicle/occupant is using a deadly weapon) and FWPD MOS 4.1.5. In an interview, show you know the state and agency rules are stricter.

**Self-quiz:** Would Scott v. Harris alone justify a pursuit in Washington?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>No. RCW 10.116.060 imposes statutory pursuit criteria (reasonable suspicion, necessity, risk balance, supervisor oversight) and limits shooting at moving vehicles.</details>

Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/550/372/

### Payton v. New York (+ Steagald v. U.S.) · 445 U.S. 573 (1980); Steagald, 451 U.S. 204 (1981) · _Wed 12/2_
**Holding.** The Fourth Amendment bars a warrantless, nonconsensual entry into a suspect's home to make a routine felony arrest absent exigent circumstances. An arrest warrant founded on probable cause carries limited authority to enter the suspect's OWN dwelling when there is reason to believe the suspect is inside. Steagald: to search a THIRD PARTY's home for the subject of an arrest warrant, you need a search warrant (absent consent or exigency).

**Why it matters for SWAT.** This is the legal basis for high-risk arrest-warrant service. Before any entry, confirm whose residence it is and the basis for believing the subject is inside. A third-party address means you need a search warrant.

**Self-quiz:** The suspect is wanted on a felony arrest warrant and is believed to be at his girlfriend's apartment. What do you need to enter?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A search warrant for her apartment (Steagald), unless there's valid consent or exigency.</details>

Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/445/573/

### Brigham City v. Stuart + WA emergency aid (State v. Schultz; State v. Boisselle) · 547 U.S. 398 (2006); 170 Wn.2d 746 (2011); 194 Wn.2d 1 (2019) · _Thu 12/3_
**Holding.** Brigham City: police may enter a home without a warrant when they have an objectively reasonable basis to believe an occupant is seriously injured or imminently threatened with serious injury. Under the Fourth Amendment the officer's subjective motive doesn't matter. Washington, art. I §7: Schultz applied the emergency-aid exception and reaffirmed that it can't be a pretext. Boisselle (2019) restated the test: (1) the officer subjectively believed an emergency required immediate assistance to protect life/property or prevent serious injury, (2) a reasonable person would believe the same, and (3) there was a reasonable basis to associate the need with the place searched. There must be a PRESENT emergency, and a pretextual entry for a criminal investigation is unlawful (evidence suppressed).

**Why it matters for SWAT.** WA is stricter than the federal rule because your subjective belief and the absence of pretext both matter. Hasty rescue/emergency entries have to be articulated as genuine aid with a present emergency, documented as such.

**Self-quiz:** What does Washington require for emergency aid that Brigham City doesn't?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Subjective belief in a present emergency and no pretext for a criminal investigation (Boisselle/Schultz), on top of objective reasonableness and a nexus to the place.</details>

Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/547/398/

### Kentucky v. King · 563 U.S. 452 (2011) · _Fri 12/4_
**Holding.** The exigent-circumstances rule applies when police do not create the exigency by engaging or threatening to engage in conduct that violates the Fourth Amendment. If officers lawfully knock and announce and occupants then begin destroying evidence, a warrantless entry to prevent that destruction is reasonable. (8-1, Alito, J.; Ginsburg, J., dissenting)

**Why it matters for SWAT.** 'Police-created exigency' comes up in knock-and-talks and in surround-and-call-outs that escalate. Ask your legal advisor/DPA how WA courts apply King under art. I §7, which is generally more protective. Don't assume the federal rule controls in WA.

**Self-quiz:** Under King, when does police conduct defeat a claimed exigency?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>When the police created the exigency by engaging or threatening to engage in conduct that violates the Fourth Amendment, e.g. threatening an unlawful entry.</details>

Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/563/452/

### Knock-and-announce: Wilson, Richards, Banks, Hudson + State v. Coyle · Wilson v. Arkansas, 514 U.S. 927 (1995); Richards v. Wisconsin, 520 U.S. 385 (1997); U.S. v. Banks, 540 U.S. 31 (2003); Hudson v. Michigan, 547 U.S. 586 (2006); State v. Coyle, 95 Wn.2d 1 (1980) · _Sat 12/5_
**Holding.** Wilson: the common-law knock-and-announce principle is part of the Fourth Amendment reasonableness inquiry, but countervailing interests can justify an unannounced entry. Richards: there's no blanket exception for felony drug cases. A no-knock entry requires reasonable suspicion that knocking would be dangerous, futile, or would inhibit the investigation (e.g. destruction of evidence), decided case by case. Banks: waiting 15-20 s before forcing entry was reasonable where cocaine could be destroyed. Hudson: a knock-and-announce violation does not trigger the exclusionary rule. Coyle (WA): RCW 10.31.040 applies whenever police enter without valid permission, not just when they 'break' in. Before a nonconsensual entry officers must announce identity, demand admittance, announce purpose, and be explicitly or implicitly refused. The statute's purposes are to reduce violence, prevent property damage and protect privacy, and exceptions are narrow.

**Why it matters for SWAT.** This is the core law of warrant service. Since 2021 (2021 c 320 §8), RCW 10.31.040(2) bars officers from seeking, and courts from issuing, warrants with an express no-knock exception. Whether an on-scene exigency can still excuse compliance is a case-law question, so learn how Valley SWAT trains it (announce, wait time, breach).

**Self-quiz:** List the four Coyle steps before a nonconsensual entry, and the three purposes of the rule.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Announce identity, demand admittance, announce purpose, be refused (explicitly or implicitly). Purposes: reduce violence, prevent property damage, protect privacy.</details>

Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/washington/supreme-court/1980/47011-1-1.html

https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=10.120&full=true
- 10.120.010 definitions: 'necessary', 'physical force', 'totality of the circumstances' (includes the officer's own actions), 'de-escalation tactics', 'less lethal alternatives'
- 10.120.020(1): the 11 lawful purposes for physical force, including (g) executing a search warrant and (i) stopping flight from a Terry stop (added by HB 2037)
- 10.120.020(2) deadly force: only when necessary to protect against an IMMEDIATE threat of serious physical injury or death (present and apparent ability, opportunity and intent)
- 10.120.020(3) reasonable care: de-escalate when possible, least force necessary, stop when necessity ends, less-lethal before deadly when possible, carry issued less-lethal
- 2022 c 4 intent: RCW 10.120.020 is a distinct, more restrictive CIVIL standard and does not abrogate the criminal protections in ch. 9A.16

### Tue 12/1: FWPD Manual of Standards Ch. 4: Use of Force (current rev. 05-18-2026), §§4.1.1-4.1.6
https://agportal-s3bucket.s3.amazonaws.com/useofforcepolicy/UseOfForcePolicy71.pdf
- Current version is rev. 05-18-2026 (your department copy). The public link here is the older rev. 03-10-2025 posted on the AG portal; when the two differ, the 05-18-2026 text controls. Section-by-section outlines of the current revision are in the Study tab (sign in)
- 4.1.1 adopts RCW 10.120.020 verbatim as FWPD policy. Note footnotes 1-2: 'possible' read as 'feasible', and 'least amount' starts above the subject's resistance

- 4.1.2 definitions (AGMUFP, chokehold, CEW, deadly force, deadly weapon, de-escalation vs. de-escalation tactics)
- 4.1.3 additional authority: RCW 9A.16.020/.040, Garner, Graham. Conflicts are resolved by deferring to RCW 10.120.020(2)
- 4.1.4 chokehold/neck restraint prohibition. Read footnote 11 (VNR in a deadly-force context under RCW 10.120.020(4))
- 4.1.5 firing at moving vehicles · 4.1.6 force includes POINTING A FIREARM at a person, plus the force factors list

### Thu 12/3: RCW 10.116: Tactics & equipment (2021 c 320 + 2023-2026 amendments)
https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=10.116&full=true
- 10.116.020 chokeholds/neck restraints prohibited · 10.116.022 hog-tying prohibited (2024 c 291) and treated as excessive force for RCW 10.93.190
- 10.116.030 TEAR GAS (CS/CN, not OC) is allowed only to alleviate a present risk of serious harm from a riot, BARRICADED SUBJECT, or HOSTAGE situation. Before use: exhaust alternatives, get SUPERVISOR authorization, ANNOUNCE intent, allow time/space to comply. A riot outside a jail also needs the highest elected official's approval. Know the definitions of 'barricaded subject' and 'hostage situation'
- 10.116.040 military equipment ban. Note that 'grenade' EXCLUDES stun/flash-bang, sting, smoke, tear-gas grenades and blast balls, and 'rifle' means .50 cal+
- 10.116.050/.052: officers must be reasonably identifiable. The 2026 face-covering ban (2026 c 115, eff. 3/19/2026) EXEMPTS SWAT protective gear needed to protect the face during SWAT duties
- 10.116.060 vehicular pursuit criteria + no firing at moving vehicles except an imminent threat from a deadly weapon (2024 c 6 / I-2113 amended)
